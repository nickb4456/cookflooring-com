Basement flooring · Rhode Island homeowners

The best basement floor starts with the concrete under it.

By Cook Flooring & Tile · Published October 10, 2026

A basement floor sits on concrete, below ground, in a state with humid summers and wet springs. Pick the product second. First find out how dry and flat the slab is, because that decides which floors will last down there.

Start with the slab

Concrete that looks dry can still pass moisture up from the ground. In a Rhode Island summer, humid air also condenses on a cool slab. Either one can end up under a new floor, so start with what you already know about the space.

Write down any water you have seen: seepage after heavy rain or snowmelt, a damp line along the walls, white powder on the concrete, a sump pump that runs often, or a musty smell in August. Deal with the source before choosing a floor. Gutters, grading, and drainage repairs sit outside a flooring estimate, and they decide whether any floor will last.

The National Wood Flooring Association's homeowner handbook describes solid wood as a floor for rooms on or above ground, and engineered wood as suitable for any level, including basements. An engineered floor still depends on a dry slab.

Carpet and many laminates suffer most after water gets in. Carpet and pad hold moisture, and a fiberboard laminate core can swell. If you want either one, ask how the product handles a damp slab before you order.

Know what waterproof covers

Waterproof on the box describes the plank. The room is a separate question. Shaw's SPC installation guidance requires a dry, flat slab and says not to install over concrete with a history of high moisture, because moisture trapped under the floor can lead to mold and mildew. Use the instructions for your exact product, since the limits differ.

Ask whether the product needs a vapor barrier over concrete, what moisture test it calls for, and who performs the test. Get those answers before the flooring is ordered.

Flat, dry, and clean before anything goes down

Floating plank floors need a flat surface. A typical manufacturer limit is about 3/16 inch over 10 feet. Basement slabs often have humps, dips, and a pitch toward a floor drain, so patching or leveling can be a real part of the job. Ask for it as its own line in the estimate.

Old paint, glue, and carpet adhesive may need to come off so patching compounds and tile mortar can bond to the concrete.

Older basements sometimes have 9-inch floor tiles or black adhesive that contain asbestos. Have suspect material tested before anyone sands, scrapes, or removes it. The EPA's asbestos guidance for homeowners advises against sanding or leveling asbestos flooring, and says new flooring can sometimes be installed over it.

Check the stairs, doors, and equipment

A new floor raises the basement by its own thickness. That shortens the bottom stair and can leave doors rubbing. Measure the bottom step and the door clearances before choosing a thick plank or a tile assembly.

Decide what happens around the furnace, water heater, washer, oil tank, and baseboard heat. Ask who moves the appliances, where the flooring stops, and how floor drains and cleanouts stay reachable.

Bring the basement's history to the walkthrough

  • Water: anything you have seen, and when it happened.
  • Existing floor: what is down now, including old tile, paint, or glue.
  • Use: playroom, office, laundry, gym, or rental.
  • Humidity: whether a dehumidifier runs in summer.
  • Photos: the stairs, doors, and equipment areas.
