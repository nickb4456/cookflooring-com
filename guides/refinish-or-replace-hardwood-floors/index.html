Should you refinish or replace your hardwood floors?

By Cook Flooring & Tile · Published October 2, 2026

A scratched wood floor is not automatically a floor that needs replacing. Start with the material, the remaining wood, and the condition underneath. Then compare the work each option actually requires.

Three different jobs, three different purposes

When you ask for a flooring estimate, be clear about the result you want. Restoring shine, changing the color, and replacing damaged boards are different requests. A useful walkthrough separates them before putting a number on the job.

Options to discuss at the walkthrough
OptionWhat to discuss
RecoatRenewing a compatible existing finish when the wood itself does not need sanding.
Sand and refinishRemoving the old finish, assessing repair areas, and choosing a new stain or finish.
ReplaceRemoving unsuitable flooring and preparing for a new product, layout, and floor height.

The National Wood Flooring Association homeowner handbook explains the distinction between renewing a finish and sanding a floor. The appropriate process depends on the actual floor, not just how dull it looks in a photo.

Identify the floor before choosing a treatment

Bring any leftover boards, product labels, or records of previous refinishing to the estimate. Solid wood, engineered wood, laminate, and wood-look vinyl can look similar from standing height. They do not accept the same restoration process.

For engineered wood, the remaining real-wood wear layer matters. The NWFA engineered-floor refinishability program identifies products with wear layers suitable for refinishing. That does not establish whether an older, already-sanded floor has enough material left. Ask the installer to inspect the existing floor and its history.

Point out loose boards, uneven areas, dark stains, and any past leak. Ask which conditions belong in the flooring scope and which need another repair first. Do not assume sanding will remove every deep stain or correct an underlying moisture problem.

When keeping the existing floor is worth exploring

If you like the board width and layout, ask for a restoration option before choosing a new floor. In a Rhode Island home with hardwood running through several connected rooms, keeping that continuity may be part of the appeal. Identify the rooms you want included and where the finished work would stop.

If damage is concentrated near a doorway or an old radiator, ask whether a local board repair is practical. Confirm how the repair will be matched and whether the surrounding floor needs refinishing. A patch should be discussed as part of the finished room, not priced as an isolated board swap.

Replacement deserves a separate scope when the existing material cannot be refinished or you want a different floor altogether. Review our hardwood installation service for the layout, preparation, and transition details to include.

Compare complete scopes, not a sanding price against a material price

  • For restoration: identify rooms, repairs, stain selection, finish products, trim work, and cleanup.
  • For replacement: identify removal, disposal, subfloor preparation, flooring supply, installation, and transitions.
  • For both: agree on furniture moving, access through the house, and how unexpected conditions will be priced before additional work starts.

Ask for the work that differs between the options to be itemized. A lower headline price is difficult to compare if one proposal leaves out removal or board repairs. Our flooring estimate checklist helps you compare the same rooms and responsibilities.

Plan room access before choosing dates

Ask when each room must be empty and when it can return to use. Walking on a finished floor, moving furniture back, and replacing rugs may have different waiting periods. Get instructions for the finish actually selected instead of relying on a general timeline.
