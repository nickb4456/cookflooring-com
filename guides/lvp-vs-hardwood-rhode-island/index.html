Choosing a new floor · Rhode Island homeowners

LVP or hardwood: which fits the way you use the room?

By Cook Flooring & Tile · Published October 2, 2026

Start with the room before falling in love with a sample. An entryway, a first-floor living room, and a basement ask different things of a floor. The best comparison includes the product, the surface underneath, and how you want to care for it.

In this guide

  2. Compare the decisions that affect the finished room

Write down what the room needs

List who uses the space, how it connects to the rest of the home, and what comes through the door. For a Rhode Island entryway, think about wet footwear and the space available for a mat. For a kitchen, note appliances and the adjoining floor. For a basement, start with the slab and any history of water entry.

Bring those details to the walkthrough along with your samples. A product that looks right in a store still needs to be suitable for its location and installation method.

Read beyond the waterproof label

A waterproof plank does not fix water entering a room or remove the need to evaluate the subfloor. Shaw's SPC installation guidance requires a suitable, dry, flat subfloor and warns about moisture beneath waterproof flooring. Use the instructions for your exact product; limits and required preparation differ.

At the walkthrough, mention damp areas, previous leaks, or a musty basement. Ask what checks are needed before installation and who handles any moisture correction. This is a decision to make before ordering flooring.

Think about the floor after the first few years

If future color changes or refinishing matter to you, discuss that when choosing hardwood. Engineered products differ in their real-wood surface layer. The NWFA refinishability program is a useful starting point, alongside the product manufacturer's specifications. Do not assume every engineered floor can be sanded.

For either material, ask how a localized repair would work in your layout. A damaged plank next to a wall and one in the middle of a room may involve different access. Ask how much spare material to retain and where to record the product name, color, and batch information.

Compare the whole installation

Price flooring with the removal, preparation, installation, trim, and disposal it needs. Include stairs and transitions explicitly. Ask who moves appliances and furniture, and whether doors or baseboards require attention when floor height changes.

Do not assume an existing floor can simply stay underneath the new one. Ask whether that approach is allowed by the selected product and whether the existing surface meets its requirements. Put the agreed preparation and any allowances in the written estimate.

Use our estimate checklist to line up the scopes. If you already have wood floors, also consider whether refinishing is an option before budgeting for removal.

Make the final choice in your own light

Set samples near the walls, cabinets, and adjoining floor. Look at them during the times you use the room, then decide which practical tradeoffs you are comfortable with. Keep the sample and product information available for the installer.
