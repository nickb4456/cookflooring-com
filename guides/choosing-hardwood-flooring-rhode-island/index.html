Choosing hardwood: brands, grades, and what fits your budget.

By Cook Flooring & Tile · Published October 15, 2026

People often ask us which hardwood brand is best. The wood species, the grade, how the board is built, and how it is installed decide how a floor looks and lasts. The name on the box matters less than any of those.

We install any brand of real hardwood

Pick your floor at any flooring store or home center and we install it, from big names like Bruce to smaller mills. If you have not chosen a product yet, bring a few samples to the walkthrough and we can talk through how each one will hold up in your rooms.

Two floors from the same brand can be very different products. A brand's budget line and its premium line may share nothing but the logo, so compare the details below instead of the name.

Five things that matter more than the brand

1. The wood species. Each species has its own hardness, grain, and color.

Common hardwood floors in Rhode Island homes
WoodWhat to expect
Red oakVery common in older Rhode Island homes. Strong grain hides everyday wear, and it takes stain evenly. It has a warm, slightly pink tone.
White oakA little harder than red oak, with a cooler tone. The usual pick for natural, light, and gray looks, and for wide planks.
HickoryVery hard, with bold color changes from board to board. Good for busy homes that want a rustic look.
MapleHard and smooth, with a quiet grain. It can take stain unevenly, so it is often left natural.
WalnutA rich dark brown, and softer than oak, so it shows dents more. Best in rooms with lighter traffic.

2. The grade. Grade describes how the boards look. Select or clear grade has few knots and even color. #1 common shows some knots and color change. #2 common, sold as character or rustic, shows the most and costs the least. Grade is about looks; all of them make a sound floor.

3. Solid or engineered. Solid hardwood is one piece of wood, usually 3/4 inch thick, and can be sanded and refinished many times. Engineered hardwood is a layer of real wood on top of a stable plywood core. It handles concrete slabs and basements better. The thickness of that top layer decides how many times it can be refinished, so ask before you buy.

4. Board width. Wide planks look great, cost more, and move more as Rhode Island swings from humid summers to dry, heated winters. Narrower boards spread that movement across more seams, so it shows less.

5. Prefinished or finished on site. Prefinished boards arrive with a factory finish, so the job is faster and the room is back in use sooner. They have small beveled edges. Boards finished on site are sanded flat and coated in place. That gives a smooth, seamless surface and any stain color, with a few more days before the room is ready.

The installation matters as much as any of these. Letting the wood adjust to the house, a flat and dry subfloor, and the right fasteners or adhesive keep a good floor from gapping or squeaking later.

Why we do not install bamboo

Bamboo is a grass, and its quality varies a lot from one maker to the next. Cheaper bamboo dents easily, and many bamboo floors react badly to the humidity swings of a Rhode Island year. Repairs and refinishing are also harder to predict. We stick to real hardwood we can stand behind. If you like a light, even look, white oak or maple gets you there.

Before you buy the wood

  • Order extra: about 5 to 10 percent more than the room size for a straight layout, and more for herringbone. Keep a spare box for future repairs.
  • Check what is underneath: concrete slabs and basements usually call for engineered hardwood. Our basement flooring guide covers why.
  • Let it adjust: the wood should sit in the house before installation, as the manufacturer directs.
  • Keep the label: the product name and lot number make a future repair much easier to match.
  • Get it in writing: a written, itemized quote should list removal, subfloor preparation, the wood, installation, and finish.
